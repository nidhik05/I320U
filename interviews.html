✦ Milestone 02, Contextual Inquiry

Contextual Inquiry.

Before we design VANTAGE, we need to understand how people already decide how to get somewhere — without introducing our own solution.

01

Research Question

Research Question

How do riders currently compare transportation options and decide which service to use for a trip?

02

Purpose

For this contextual inquiry, our goal is to understand how people currently make transportation decisions when they need to get somewhere. We are focusing on what people already do, including which transportation options they consider, which apps or tools they use, what information they compare, and what factors influence their final decision.

At this stage, we are not testing VANTAGE. We want to first understand participants' existing habits, behaviors, workarounds, and decision-making processes without introducing our own solution.

03

Shared Interview Protocol

All team members use the same general protocol and baseline questions so that our interviews are consistent enough to compare later. However, the interviews are semi-structured. This means we do not have to ask every question in the exact same order, and we can ask additional follow-up questions depending on what a participant says or does.

Procedure

  1. Introduce the study and explain that we are trying to understand how people currently decide how to get somewhere.
  2. Explain that there are no right or wrong answers and that we are interested in what the participant would naturally do.
  3. Give the participant a realistic transportation scenario.
  4. Ask the participant to think out loud while deciding how they would get to the destination.
  5. Allow the participant to use any apps, websites, messages, or other tools they would normally use.
  6. Observe the participant without directing them toward a specific transportation option or service.
  7. Ask follow-up questions based on what the participant actually does.
  8. Take notes on behaviors, hesitation, workarounds, decision points, comparisons, and comments.
  9. After the task, ask a few broader questions about the participant's usual transportation habits.
  10. Keep all participant information anonymous by using participant IDs instead of names.

Baseline Interview Questions

  1. How often do you use rideshare or other transportation apps?
  2. What transportation services do you normally use?
  3. When you need to get somewhere and are not driving, how do you usually decide what transportation to take?
  4. What option do you normally check first?
  5. What information do you look at first?
  6. Do you usually compare more than one transportation option?
  7. What makes you choose one service over another?
  8. How important is price when you are deciding?
  9. How important is pickup or wait time?
  10. Does your process change when you are traveling with other people?
  11. Are there transportation services you sometimes forget about or choose not to check?
  12. What parts of choosing transportation feel inconvenient or take the most effort?
  13. Have you developed any shortcuts or workarounds when comparing transportation options?
  14. What makes you feel confident that you chose the right option?
  15. If you could change one thing about your current transportation decision-making process, what would it be?

Possible Follow-up Questions

These are not meant to be asked during every interview. They are used depending on what the participant actually says or does.

  • What made you check that first?
  • Why did you switch to another app?
  • What are you comparing right now?
  • What made you rule that option out?
  • Why does that matter to you?
  • Is that something you normally do?
  • Would your decision change if the price were different?
  • What would make you choose the other option?
  • I noticed you went back to the previous app. What were you trying to remember?
  • How would this process change if you were traveling with more people?
  • What would you normally do next?
04

Contextual Inquiry Planning

Team Checklist

  • Finalize the main research question.
  • Finalize the shared interview protocol.
  • Finalize the baseline interview questions.
  • Each team member recruits two participants.
  • Each team member conducts two interview/observations.
  • Use participant IDs to keep interview data anonymous.
  • Take detailed notes during each interview.
  • Avoid discussing conclusions from an interview before the interpretation session.
  • Hold the interpretation session as soon as possible after the interviews, ideally within 48 hours.
  • Bring interview notes and observations to the interpretation session.
  • Review interview evidence together as a team.
  • Turn important observations into affinity notes.
  • Organize affinity notes into related groups.
  • Identify larger patterns based on the grouped notes.
  • Create the final affinity diagram.
  • Check that each affinity note can be traced back to actual interview evidence.
  • Add a legible version of the final affinity diagram to the website.
  • Make sure documentation from every team member's two interviews is included on the website.
05

Interpretation Session

After completing the interviews, our team will meet for an interpretation session. The purpose of the session is to bring together the information collected during the individual interviews and make sense of it as a group. Instead of each person deciding on their own what the main findings are, we will review the interview evidence together and discuss important behaviors, decisions, needs, workarounds, frustrations, and patterns that appeared during the interviews. During the interpretation session, we will turn specific observations into affinity notes. Each note should represent one idea and should stay connected to something that was actually observed or said during an interview.

We will then combine affinity notes from all team members and organize related notes together. This will help us identify patterns across participants without deciding on categories too early.

06

Affinity Diagram

The affinity diagram will be created as a team using the affinity notes collected from everyone's interviews. Each team member will bring notes from their two interview/observations into the interpretation session. We will then group similar notes based on patterns that naturally appear across the research. The goal is not to force the notes into categories we decide on ahead of time. Instead, the larger themes should emerge from the interview data. The final website will include a legible version of the completed affinity diagram along with the individual interview documentation that supports it.

Key Findings

Across both interviews, Uber was the automatic first stop, with Lyft used only as a reactive check when the first price felt high — and both participants lost track of fares and pickup times while switching between apps, relying on memory rather than writing anything down. Price was never evaluated in isolation: P03 traded a cheaper Lyft fare for a faster Uber because she could not risk being late, while P02 evaluated every price per person rather than as a total, which changed how "expensive" a fare felt once a group was splitting it. Less familiar options (Fetii, robotaxis, the bus) were only considered when convenient or prompted by someone else, and in P02's case Fetii was completely forgotten until a friend brought it up. Both participants stopped comparing the moment they reached an option that felt "good enough" for the specific constraint driving that trip — time pressure for P03, group cohesion for P02 — rather than continuing to search for the objectively cheapest option.

Common services: Uber, Lyft, Google Maps, group chats, and occasionally hears friends suggest Fetii.
Main priorities: Getting the whole group together, leaving without a long delay, having enough passenger space, and keeping the cost per person reasonable.

Scenario: The participant is in a West Campus apartment with five friends on a Friday night. The group wants to go downtown around 10:15 PM. Nobody wants to drive because they expect to drink, and the participant is one of the people trying to figure out transportation for the group. Everyone is getting ready at the same time, so the decision is slightly rushed.

Interview & Observation Notes
  1. The participant opens Uber almost immediately and says that is normally what the group does when they go downtown.
  2. Instead of selecting a regular Uber, they scroll to the larger vehicle options because there are six people.
  3. Their first reaction is to the Uber XL price. They say the total looks expensive but then immediately start thinking about how much it would be per person.
  4. They divide the total by six using rough mental math instead of opening a calculator.
  5. They tell the group the approximate price per person rather than the total ride price.
  6. One friend asks whether Lyft is cheaper, so the participant opens Lyft while keeping Uber open in the background.
  7. They enter the same destination in Lyft and go directly to the XL option instead of looking at standard rides.
  8. Lyft is slightly cheaper, but the pickup time is longer.
  9. The participant goes back to Uber to compare the wait and says they care more about leaving soon because everyone is already ready.
  10. They have forgotten the exact Lyft pickup time, so they switch to Lyft again to double-check it.
  11. Another friend asks if they should just get two normal Ubers instead of one XL.
  12. The participant compares the price of two regular Ubers with one XL and realizes that the difference is not very large.
  13. They prefer keeping everyone in one car because coordinating two pickups sounds annoying.
  14. They also say that when the group splits into two cars, one car sometimes gets downtown much earlier than the other.
  15. A friend mentions Fetii because the group used it once with other people. The participant says, "Oh yeah, I forgot about that."
  16. The participant does not have Fetii downloaded and asks the friend who mentioned it whether they know how much it usually costs.
  17. The friend is not sure, and because everyone is ready to leave, the participant does not download the app or create an account to check.
  18. The group briefly talks about taking the bus downtown because it would be cheaper.
  19. The participant opens Google Maps to look at the route but seems less familiar with the transit information than with Uber or Lyft.
  20. They notice the bus would require walking to a stop and waiting. They say it is possible but nobody in the group seems excited about doing it.
  21. One person suggests walking all the way downtown. The participant checks the estimated walk time and immediately rejects it because the group does not want to arrive sweaty and tired.
  22. The participant does not consider driving because nobody in the group wants to worry about parking or having a designated driver.
  23. They mention robotaxis when one passes in conversation, but they are unsure whether six people could ride together. Because of the group size, they do not investigate it further.
  24. When Uber's price looks higher than the participant expected, they wait around a minute and refresh once to see whether the price changes.
  25. The price barely changes, and the group eventually chooses the Uber XL because it can take everyone together and has the fastest reasonable pickup time, even though it is not the cheapest possible option.

Key Findings

Both of Zach's participants check a non-rideshare option first — P07 opens Google Maps out of habit to see if the bus is realistic before paying for anything, and P08's group briefly discusses the bus before defaulting to Uber — but both ultimately treat rideshare as the real option once time of night or group size is factored in. Price comparisons in both interviews are undermined by memory: P07 forgets the exact Uber price and pickup time while checking Lyft and has to switch back, and P08 forgets the Lyft pickup time the same way. For P07 alone at night, safety and fatigue outweigh a $2–3 savings; for P08 in a group, the deciding factor is fit and group cohesion rather than the lowest per-person price. In both cases, Fetii is only considered when prompted (P07 doesn't think of it at all solo, P08 only remembers it after a friend mentions it and drops it once the price isn't readily available), reinforcing that less-habitual services need to be surfaced, not just available, to get considered.
