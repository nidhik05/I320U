✦ Milestone 03, Personas

Personas.

Who VANTAGE is designed for, who it also has to work for, and who it is deliberately not for.

Every persona is fictional. Each behavior pattern is built from our contextual inquiry and quotes the top-level label of the affinity diagram it comes from, along with the interview notes behind it.

01

Method

We followed Cooper et al. (2014), chapter 3, and Pruitt and Grudin (2003). Instead of a separate persona study, our research input is the Milestone 2 contextual inquiry: eight participants (P01 to P08), two observed by each team member. We started from the key issues at the top of the affinity diagram, grouped participants by the behavior patterns those issues describe, and built one persona per distinct cluster of goals and means.

Each behavior pattern below includes:

  • Key issue: the exact wording of the top-level (green) label in our affinity diagram.
  • Sub-group: the second- or third-level label underneath it, when it is more specific.
  • Notes: the interview note IDs (participant and note number) that support the pattern.

The diagram has 14 top-level labels. Seven of them drive the persona behaviors below; the rest (for example "Transportation decisions incorporate social and practical consequences beyond the ride itself") mostly describe behaviors we chose to put out of scope in section 05.

02

Primary Persona

Maya Chen, the everyday comparer

Primary · Prototype focus
21 · College student · Austin, TX · Uses Uber, Lyft, occasional public transit

"I usually check Uber first, but I'll compare Lyft to see if I can get a better price or faster pickup."

Representative quote based on our interview findings, not a direct participant quote.

Narrative

Maya is a college student in Austin who regularly uses rideshare to get around, especially when she is running late or traveling at night. She typically opens Uber first because she is more familiar with it, but will also check Lyft to see if there is a cheaper option or a faster pickup. Since both apps show their information separately, she has to go back and forth between them to compare.

Maya wants to save money, but she knows the cheapest option is not always the best one. If she is running late, she would rather pay a few extra dollars for a ride that arrives sooner. When she is traveling alone at night, she puts safety and convenience ahead of price. She sometimes considers the bus, but is less likely to take it if it means extra walking or waiting outside.

Goals

  • Compare transportation options in one place without switching between apps.
  • Find affordable rides while still weighing pickup time and total travel time.
  • Feel safe and comfortable with her choice, especially alone or at night.
  • Spend less time comparing and feel confident she picked the right option for the situation.

Behavior patterns

  1. Starts with the app she already knows. Maya opens Uber first because it is the app she is most familiar with, then checks Lyft when the first price seems high.
    Key issue: "Familiarity and existing habits shape which transportation options riders consider"
    Sub-group: "Riders usually begin with the transportation service already in their routine"
    Notes: P01-01, P03-01, P03-02, P01-10, P01-11
  2. Compares prices and pickup times across apps, and has to remember them. She switches between Uber and Lyft and has to hold the previous app's price and wait in her head, which makes the comparison more frustrating than it needs to be.
    Key issue: "Comparing transportation across separate apps creates memory and coordination work"
    Sub-group: "Switching between apps makes information harder to remember"
    Notes: P03-16, P03-17, P05-12, P06-16, P08-17
  3. Pays more when she is running late. She prefers cheaper rides, but when she is late she prioritizes a shorter pickup over saving a few dollars.
    Key issue: "Time pressure and trip context change which transportation attributes matter most"
    Sub-group: "Urgent trips shift attention toward pickup and arrival time"
    Notes: P03-04, P03-30, P03-33, P04-06, P04-08
  4. Puts safety first at night. Alone at night, she cares less about the cheapest option and more about getting home safely, and she is less likely to walk or wait outside for a bus.
    Key issue: "Safety, comfort, and environmental conditions change transportation decisions, especially at night"
    Sub-group: "Time of day changes which transportation options feel acceptable"
    Notes: P01-35, P07-06, P07-07, P05-39
  5. Stops once an option is good enough. Once she finds a reasonable price and pickup time, she books it instead of checking every available service.
    Key issue: "Riders stop comparing once an option feels acceptable rather than searching for a perfect choice"
    Sub-group: "Riders use practical stopping points during comparison"
    Notes: P04-36, P05-37, P06-40, P03-37

Pain points

  • Switching between Uber and Lyft just to compare prices and pickup times.
  • Remembering prices and waits from one app while looking at another.
  • Not knowing whether saving a few dollars is worth a longer wait.
  • Priorities that change depending on whether she is late, alone, or out at night.

Why Maya is the primary persona

Maya represents the most widespread problem we observed: switching between separate apps to compare options for the same trip. Participants were not just looking for the cheapest ride. They weighed price, pickup time, convenience, and safety differently depending on the situation, and Maya's behavior changes in the same way. The prototype and its scenarios are designed for her first. The secondary personas add needs that the prototype has to accommodate without losing focus on Maya.

Design implications

  • Side-by-side comparison of options in one view, so she never has to remember a number from another app.
  • Price, pickup time, and total travel time together, since the cheapest option is not always the right one.
  • Sorting by what matters right now: cheapest or fastest.
  • A simple interface that does not add another complicated step to a decision she wants to finish quickly.

Primary scenario

Maya finishes studying on campus at 10:30 PM and needs to get home. Uber shows $19 with a six-minute pickup; Lyft shows $17 with a nine-minute wait. She is traveling alone at night and wonders whether saving $2 is worth three more minutes outside. Instead of flipping between both apps and trying to remember the estimates, she opens VANTAGE, sees both options side by side with price, pickup time, and total trip time, and books the one that fits her priorities that night.

03

Secondary Personas

Daniel Kim, the group ride organizer

Secondary 1
21 · College student · Austin, TX · Books rides for groups of friends

"I want to find a ride that works for everyone without spending forever comparing options."

Representative quote, not a direct participant quote.

Narrative

Daniel is usually the one who plans the ride when he goes out with friends. He checks how many people need a ride, whether they fit in one car, and how much each person would pay. He checks Uber first because he knows the app, and looks at Lyft or other options if the price is high. He wants to decide quickly so the group isn't left waiting.

Goals

  • Find a ride that fits everyone in the group.
  • Keep the cost per person reasonable.
  • Balance price, pickup time, and the group's schedule.
  • Avoid switching back and forth between apps.

Behavior patterns

  1. Checks how many people fit in a car. He compares one larger vehicle with splitting the group into separate cars.
    Key issue: "Group transportation decisions center on capacity, staying together, and shared cost"
    Sub-group: "Groups compare one large vehicle with splitting into multiple cars"
    Notes: P01-20, P01-21, P02-20, P02-21
  2. Thinks in cost per person. He looks past the total fare to how it would split among friends.
    Key issue: "Group transportation decisions center on capacity, staying together, and shared cost"
    Sub-group: "Riders translate group fares into per-person cost" → "Mental division is part of evaluating whether a group fare is reasonable"
    Notes: P01-08, P02-33, P06-06
  3. Opens a familiar app first, then checks others. He starts with Uber, and looks at Lyft or other services if the fare seems too high.
    Key issue: "Familiarity and existing habits shape which transportation options riders consider"
    Sub-group: "Riders usually begin with the transportation service already in their routine"
    Notes: P01-01, P02-02, P08-01, P01-10
  4. Lets group readiness set the timing, and rechecks before booking. If the group is still getting ready, a fast pickup matters less; if the fare changes in the meantime, he compares again.
    Key issue: "Group and individual trips have different transportation decision structures"
    Sub-group: "Group readiness affects whether a faster pickup actually matters"
    Notes: P06-09, P06-10
    Also: "Comparing transportation across separate apps creates memory and coordination work" → "Riders return to apps to recheck information" (P01-36)

How Daniel differs from Maya

Maya chooses the best ride for her own trip. Daniel is choosing for other people too, so his means include group size, space in the car, the per-person split, and when everyone is actually ready. The prototype should show capacity and cost per person next to price and pickup time, without turning into a group-payment tool.

Elena Torres, the multimodal planner

Secondary 2
19 · Sophomore · Lives off Riverside, about 20 minutes from campus by bus · No car · Uses Google Maps, Uber, Lyft; has CapMetro but rarely opens it
By Zach Smith

Narrative

Elena moved off campus last year and still doesn't have a car. Her student ID gets her on the bus for free, so that's how she gets almost everywhere during the day. When she needs to go somewhere, she opens Google Maps first. If a bus is coming soon, she's done deciding. If not, she opens Uber, sometimes Lyft, and the question stops being "which bus" and becomes "is this worth twenty dollars."

Her answer depends on the clock. At 2 PM she will wait fifteen minutes at a stop to keep the money. At 11:30 PM, walking out of a study session, she won't stand outside alone that long, so she pays. For trips she can't be late to, like a doctor's appointment or an early flight, she sorts it out the day before so she isn't deciding in a rush. Elena wouldn't describe herself as someone who compares rides. She takes the bus, except when she can't.

Goals

  • Pay for a ride only when the free option really doesn't work.
  • Get places on time without padding every trip by half an hour.
  • Avoid waiting outside alone at night.
  • Find out the bus won't work before she is standing at the stop.

Behavior patterns

  1. Starts in Google Maps and treats rideshare as the fallback. She checks transit and walking estimates first. A rideshare app only gets opened once the bus looks too slow or too far off, and it's Uber, because that's the one she knows.
    Key issue: "Public transit, walking, and driving remain alternatives, but their usefulness depends heavily on context"
    Sub-group: "Riders use Google Maps to investigate whether transit is realistic"
    Notes: P07-01, P07-02, P04-12, P08-32, P03-19
    Also: "Familiarity and existing habits shape which transportation options riders consider" (P07-10)
  2. Lets the time of day and how much slack she has pick the mode. In the afternoon she'll wait for a bus, walk part of the way, or try a Waymo. At night, or when she can't be late, she switches to Uber or Lyft and accepts paying a few extra dollars.
    Key issue: "Time pressure and trip context change which transportation attributes matter most"
    Sub-group: "When riders have flexibility, longer waits become more acceptable"
    Notes: P04-15, P03-22, P03-30
    Also: "Safety, comfort, and environmental conditions change transportation decisions, especially at night" → "Time of day changes which transportation options feel acceptable" (P07-06, P07-07, P07-33, P05-39)
  3. Juggles estimates from three apps in her head and loses track. She goes from Maps to Uber to Lyft and back to Maps, retypes her address in the app where it isn't saved, and is never sure whether a fare changed or she just remembered it wrong.
    Key issue: "Comparing transportation across separate apps creates memory and coordination work"
    Sub-groups: "Switching between apps makes information harder to remember", "Differences between apps create additional effort"
    Notes: P07-19, P07-22, P07-24, P03-15
    Also: "Changing prices, pickup estimates, and reliability create uncertainty during the decision" (P07-26, P07-27)
  4. Skips services outside her routine unless the situation calls for them. Fetii doesn't register on a solo trip because she thinks of it as a group van. Robotaxis are a daytime, low-stakes option she passes on at night or when timing is tight.
    Key issue: "Familiarity and existing habits shape which transportation options riders consider"
    Sub-group: "Riders associate some services with specific types of trips"
    Notes: P07-35, P07-39, P04-29, P04-30, P04-33, P05-24
    Also: "Newer transportation options can create additional comfort concerns" (P05-25)

How Elena differs from Maya

Maya chooses between rideshare apps. Elena chooses between ways of getting there, and rideshare is the one she would rather not pay for. Their goals overlap on price and arriving on time, but the means differ: she starts in Google Maps instead of Uber, her cheap option is a free bus rather than the lower of two fares, and the wait she weighs includes standing at a stop. A screen that only lines Uber up against Lyft misses her first question, which is whether she needs to pay for a ride at all.

05

Out of Scope: Anti-Persona

Derek, the set-and-forget driver

Anti-persona
24 · First-year grad student · Has a car and drives almost everywhere

Derek drives himself almost everywhere, including downtown, where he pays for a garage. The only time he takes a ride is to the airport, and his department reimburses it, so he books Lyft without looking at anything else. If he wanted a ride tool at all, he would want it to book the car, store his card, and send an expense receipt.

Derek is a reasonable person. VANTAGE is not for him, and designing for him would pull us away from Maya and the secondary personas.

Behaviors we are choosing not to serve

  • Deciding whether to drive. Participants did weigh driving (P04-01, P02-01, P06-22), but every time it was ruled out before any comparing started. VANTAGE picks up after someone has already decided not to drive.
  • Riders who never compare because price doesn't matter to them. Someone on a company card, or someone fully loyal to one app, has nothing to gain from a comparison. Our own pivot criteria say that if most students behaved this way, comparison would not be the problem to solve.
  • Booking, payment, and collecting money afterward. Group payers in our interviews covered the fare and chased Venmo later (P06-29, P01-39). That pain is real, and it shows up under "Transportation decisions incorporate social and practical consequences beyond the ride itself", but our scope already leaves out payments, accounts, and credentials.
  • Anything after the ride is booked. Tracking the driver, checking the plate, sharing trip status, and tipping (P05-27, P05-28, P05-29, P03-38) are handled well inside the rideshare apps, and participants stopped comparing the moment they booked.
  • Drivers, non-UT riders, and other cities. Same exclusions as our Milestone 1 scope.
